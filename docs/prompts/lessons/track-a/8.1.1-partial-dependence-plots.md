## Required conceptual and mathematical coverage

### 1. Motivation and intuition

Introduce the problem: a predictive model may be accurate but difficult to understand. Explain the global question: how do predictions change on average as one feature changes while the distribution of the other features is integrated out?

Use a small intuitive example, such as a model predicting house prices while examining floor area. Explicitly distinguish:
- observed target values;
- model predictions;
- the feature being varied;
- the other features being averaged over;
- the population/data distribution represented by the reference dataset.

### 2. Formal definition

Define a model (f(x)), a feature of interest (X_j), and the remaining features (X_{-j}). Present and explain:

[
PD_j(z)=mathbb{E}_{X_{-j}}left[f(z,X_{-j})ight].
]

Explain each symbol and the intervention-like computational operation carefully: set the feature of interest to (z) for reference observations, retain their other feature values, obtain predictions, then average those predictions.

Make clear that this is a model-based averaging operation, not a causal intervention and not an estimate of a causal effect.

### 3. Empirical estimator

Present the finite-sample estimator:

[
widehat{PD}_j(z)=rac{1}{n}sum_{i=1}^{n}f(z,x_{i,-j}).
]

Explain the algorithm step by step:
1. select a reference dataset;
2. choose a grid of values (z) for the feature of interest;
3. copy the reference observations for each grid value;
4. replace feature (j) with (z);
5. evaluate the model;
6. average predictions across reference observations;
7. plot the resulting averages against (z).

Discuss grid selection, computational cost, reference-sample representativeness, and model output scale (regression, probability, or another prediction target). Avoid suggesting that all libraries expose identical defaults.

### 4. Two-feature PDP

Explain the two-feature extension:

[
PD_{j,k}(z_j,z_k)=mathbb{E}_{X_{-(j,k)}}left[f(z_j,z_k,X_{-(j,k)})ight].
]

Describe surface, contour, and heatmap representations, including how to read interactions and why two-dimensional plots become harder to interpret as feature count grows.

### 5. Interpretation

Teach learners to interpret:
- increasing, decreasing, flat, nonlinear, and threshold-like patterns;
- prediction units and output scale;
- average trends versus heterogeneous individual responses;
- behavior near sparse regions of the feature distribution;
- the distinction between a smooth visual curve and strong empirical support.

Use a worked example and explain the interpretation without overclaiming.

### 6. Limitations and feature dependence

This must be a substantial section, not a brief disclaimer.

Explain that the PDP calculation may create feature combinations that are rare or absent in the observed data when the feature of interest is correlated/dependent on other features. Such combinations can cause the model to be evaluated off the data manifold and can produce misleading curves.

Use a clear example (for instance, age and years of work experience, or house area and number of rooms). Explain:
- why the combinations are implausible;
- how the model may behave in unsupported regions;
- why the average can conceal heterogeneous responses;
- why PDP does not establish causation;
- how to inspect feature distributions and dependence;
- when ICE or ALE is a better diagnostic or alternative.

Do not claim PDP is always invalid with correlated features; explain the conditions and risks accurately.

### 7. PDP, ICE, and ALE comparison

Include a compact comparison table or equivalent accessible layout.

- PDP: average model response over the reference distribution; global summary; can hide heterogeneity and be affected by unrealistic combinations.
- ICE: individual conditional response curves; reveals heterogeneity; many curves may clutter the plot and it shares off-manifold concerns with PDP.
- ALE: accumulates local effects over observed feature intervals; generally reduces extrapolation into unrealistic combinations under feature dependence; interpretation and implementation have their own assumptions and nuances.

Explain how this lesson prepares the learner for 8.1.2 ICE and 8.1.3 ALE. Do not fully replace those future lessons.
