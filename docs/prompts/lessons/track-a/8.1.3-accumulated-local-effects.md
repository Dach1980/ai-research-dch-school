## Task

Create the complete lesson **8.1.3 — ALE — Accumulated Local Effects** for AI Research DCH School in Russian, English, and Simplified Chinese.

Teach learners how Accumulated Local Effects estimate and visualize a model's local feature effects over the observed feature distribution, why this can reduce the unrealistic-combination problem associated with PDP/ICE when predictors are dependent, and how to calculate, interpret, validate, and critically assess first-order ALE plots.

The key progression is **PDP → ICE → ALE**:
- PDP summarizes an average prediction response and may evaluate unsupported combinations.
- ICE exposes heterogeneous individual responses but retains the same one-feature-at-a-time off-manifold concern.
- ALE accumulates local prediction differences within intervals of the feature's observed distribution, reducing reliance on distant or unrealistic combinations.

Do not present ALE as universally unbiased, causal, assumption-free, or immune to extrapolation. Explain exactly what it estimates and where its limitations remain. Do not repeat the PDP and ICE lessons; build on them and compare methods where needed.

## 3. Required conceptual and mathematical content

### 3.1 Motivation: why local effects?

Start with a concrete example such as a house-price model where floor area and number of rooms are dependent, or a salary model where age and years of experience are dependent. Explain how PDP/ICE can combine a value of one feature with fixed values of other features that are rare or absent in the data.

Explain ALE's intuition: instead of querying the model at arbitrary distant feature values for every reference row, estimate local prediction changes over narrow intervals where observations with the relevant feature values occur, then accumulate those changes.

Distinguish observed data and its joint distribution, the fitted prediction function, a local prediction difference, a conditional average within a feature interval, accumulated and centered effects, and a causal intervention (which ALE is not).

### 3.3 Small worked example

Use a small, fully specified numerical example with a few intervals and observations. Show interval boundaries, observations assigned to each interval, prediction differences for each observation at the two boundaries, average local difference per interval, cumulative sum, centering constant, and centered ALE values.

Check arithmetic carefully. Make clear that the toy numbers illustrate the estimator, not a causal effect. Include enough intermediate values for a learner to reproduce the calculation.

### 3.4 Reading an ALE plot

Explain:
- x-axis: observed feature values / interval boundaries;
- y-axis: centered accumulated model effect in the model output's units or scale;
- zero: a centered reference level, not “no effect everywhere” and not a prediction of zero;
- increasing/decreasing segments: local accumulated changes in model output;
- flat segments: little estimated local change over that range;
- nonlinear and threshold-like patterns;
- irregular/noisy patterns and sparse intervals;
- why vertical differences are relative effects, not raw predicted outcomes.

Discuss the prediction scale: regression output, decision function, probability, or another scalar output. State that ALE is defined for the chosen prediction function; probability-scale curves can differ from logit/decision-score curves.

### 3.5 Why ALE helps with feature dependence — and what it does not solve

Explain precisely why ALE generally reduces the off-manifold problem compared with PDP/ICE: local differences are calculated for observations whose feature values fall in the relevant interval, retaining their observed values of other predictors. It does not combine every reference row with every grid value across the full feature range.

Use a two-feature example and show how the local approach follows the observed conditional distribution more closely.

Do not overstate:
- ALE still evaluates interval-boundary substitutions and can create locally unsupported combinations;
- wide intervals, sparse bins, strong nonlinearities, or poor bin placement can cause error;
- conditional distributions can be complex and vary within a bin;
- ALE does not remove confounding, establish causality, or explain why the data distribution arose;
- strong feature dependence can make individual feature effects difficult to identify or interpret;
- interactions can complicate first-order summaries.

Compare limitations against PDP and ICE in a balanced way.

### 3.6 Binning and estimation choices

Explain quantile-based versus equally spaced intervals; why quantile bins often distribute observations more evenly; trade-offs between number of bins, local resolution, variance, and stability; empty or sparsely populated bins and how to handle/report them; duplicate quantile boundaries for discrete/repeated values; sample size and uncertainty; sensitivity analysis across reasonable bin counts; outliers and feature-range selection; train/test/reference data policy and avoiding leakage; and prediction batching/computational cost.

Do not prescribe a universal number of bins. Recommend checking bin counts and stability, and reporting implementation choices.

### 3.7 Two-dimensional ALE and interactions

Introduce second-order / two-feature ALE conceptually. Explain that it can reveal local interaction structure not represented by first-order ALE curves. Explain why it is more demanding: joint bins, sparse cells, centering conventions, interpretation, and separation of main effects from interactions require care.

A full production implementation of second-order ALE is not mandatory if it distracts from first-order ALE. Include a clear visual or conceptual illustration and explain when a two-feature analysis is justified.

### 3.8 Comparison: PDP, ICE, and ALE

Include an accessible comparison table:

| Method | What is summarized? | Main strength | Main limitation / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| PDP | Average predictions after setting the selected feature to grid values | Simple global response summary | May average over unrealistic combinations and hide heterogeneity |
| ICE | Individual prediction curves under the same feature replacement | Reveals heterogeneous individual responses | Visual clutter; inherits off-manifold combinations |
| ALE | Accumulated local prediction differences over observed feature intervals | Usually reduces extrapolation caused by feature dependence | Sensitive to bins/sparsity; centered relative effects; not causal |

Explain how to choose:
- PDP when a global average prediction response is meaningful and dependence concerns are limited or explicitly checked;
- ICE when individual heterogeneity is the main question and sampled replacement combinations are interpretable;
- ALE when feature dependence makes global replacement-based methods problematic and local observed-region effects are the target.

Emphasize that the plots answer different questions and should be interpreted alongside feature distributions and domain knowledge.
