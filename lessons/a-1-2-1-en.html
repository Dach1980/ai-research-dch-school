What Is Data in Machine Learning?

Data are more than a table or a file. They are the result of observations, measurements, and collection rules that shape the machine-learning problem.

1.2.1
RUEN中文

Learning outcomes

  • Explain data, an observation, and a dataset.
  • Distinguish structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data.
  • Describe a dataset’s provenance, units, time span, and limitations.
  • Explain why conclusions depend on collection methods and representativeness.

Lesson content

1. Data as evidence about the world

In machine learning, data are recorded information about objects, events, or processes. A temperature reading, a support message, an image of a component, and a transaction record are different forms of data. A large file does not automatically contain information suitable for a particular task.

Distinguish the real-world object from its digital representation. A sensor does not measure “temperature in general”; it records a quantity at a particular place and time, with a measurement error and a defined procedure.

2. An observation and a dataset

An observation is one recorded unit of study: a flight, a property, one day of a building’s operation, or one image. A dataset combines observations under consistent rules.

Define the unit of observation before analysis. If one row represents a day, do not later treat it as a separate building. Repeated measurements from the same entity create dependence between rows, which matters when data are split.

3. Forms of data

Structured data follow a predefined schema of rows, columns, and field types. Semi-structured data retain recurring organization while allowing variable fields, as in JSON events. Unstructured data do not fit a simple table: text, images, audio, and video.

A project often combines several forms: a metadata table, sensor images, and text logs. Before training, they must be linked by identifiers and time, with those joins checked for correctness.

4. Where data come from and what they mean

Sources include sensors, information systems, experiments, surveys, open datasets, and human annotation. A dataset card should record the owner and source, collection period, geography, sampling frequency, units, inclusion and exclusion rules, and known process changes.

Metric definitions matter. “Daily energy consumption” and “instantaneous power” are not interchangeable. Numbers without units, time zones, and measurement methods can lead to incorrect conclusions.

5. Representativeness and sampling bias

Data should reflect the conditions in which a model will be used. A model intended for all buildings but trained only on offices in one city may fail on schools, hospitals, or another climate. This is sampling bias, not a problem automatically solved by a more complex algorithm.

A large volume does not guarantee representativeness: a million repeated records from one narrow segment may contain less useful diversity than a smaller, well-designed sample. Compare training data with the future deployment population.

6. Practice: create a dataset profile

Choose a task, such as forecasting a building’s daily energy consumption. Record what one row represents; who measures each variable and how; the collection period; which buildings and operating modes are represented; the units; and which cases were excluded.

List three possible limitations: faulty sensors, missing weekends, changed operating schedules, or under-representation of some building types. For each, state which conclusions it could affect.
