Correlation, Relationships, and Data Leakage

Relationships between features help us explore data, but correlation does not establish causation. Data leakage can also make model evaluation look unrealistically strong.

1.2.6
RUEN中文

Learning outcomes

  • Distinguish positive, negative, and near-zero linear correlation.
  • Explain the limits of correlation and the difference between association and causation.
  • Recognize confounding variables and nonlinear relationships.
  • Identify target, temporal, duplicate, and preprocessing leakage.

Lesson content

1. What correlation tells us

Correlation describes how two variables vary together. A positive association means larger values of one variable tend to occur with larger values of the other; a negative association means one tends to decrease as the other increases.

Pearson’s coefficient measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship and ranges from −1 to +1. A value near zero indicates little linear association, but it does not rule out a nonlinear relationship.

2. Correlation is not causation

If ice-cream sales and sunburn cases both rise in summer, one does not necessarily cause the other. Temperature and sunlight may influence both variables; this is a confounder.

Causal conclusions require a study design, temporal ordering, consideration of alternative explanations, and, where possible, an experiment or a justified quasi-experiment. A correlation matrix alone does not identify a mechanism.

3. Linear and nonlinear relationships

Pearson correlation can miss a strong nonlinear pattern. For example, an outcome may be lowest at moderate temperatures and rise at both very low and very high temperatures. A scatter plot helps reveal the shape.

Spearman correlation uses ranks to assess monotonic association and can be useful for nonlinear but monotonic patterns or data with outliers. Neither coefficient replaces visualization, domain knowledge, or temporal analysis.

4. What data leakage means

Leakage occurs when training or evaluation uses information that would not be available at the real prediction time, or when information from an evaluation set influences fitting. The result is an inflated metric and false confidence.

Examples include a feature derived from a future target value, random splitting that separates repeated records of one entity, normalization before splitting, or a field recording a decision made after the event being predicted.

5. Leakage types and safeguards

Target leakage: an input directly or indirectly contains the answer. Temporal leakage: future information enters training relative to the prediction point. Duplicate/group leakage: near-identical records cross the split. Preprocessing leakage: statistics or feature selection are computed on the full dataset.

Start by defining the prediction time and when each feature becomes available. Use temporal or group-aware splits and a preprocessing pipeline fitted only on train. Check whether the target itself is constructed from information unavailable in deployment.

6. Practice: inspect a suspicious relationship

Task: predict an equipment shutdown one day before it happens. The table contains temperature, vibration, a maintenance plan, an incident code, and the actual shutdown time. The incident code and actual time are recorded only after the event.

Define the prediction time. Mark which fields are available beforehand and which reveal the future. Explain why a high correlation with the target is not enough to justify including a field.
