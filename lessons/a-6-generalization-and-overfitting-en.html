TRACK A · MODULE 11 · A.6

Why Does a Model Fail on New Data?

Generalization, underfitting and overfitting: why strong performance on training examples does not guarantee success in real use.

After this lesson, you will be able to

  • Explain generalization and distinguish learning a pattern from memorization.
  • Identify underfitting, useful fit and overfitting from training and validation errors.
  • Read error curves as model complexity and training-set size change.
  • Choose ways to improve a model: regularization, early stopping, more data and sound validation.

Study time: 60–75 minutes · beginner

1. From sample quality to generalization

In A.5, we learned to measure quality and separate training, validation and test sets. Now ask the key question: will the model work on data it has never seen? The ability to capture stable patterns and maintain performance on new examples is called generalization.

Imagine predicting a building's energy consumption from floor area, equipment and weather. If a model predicts only buildings in its training set well but fails on a new building, it has not learned a transferable rule. The goal is not to reproduce past answers; it is to build a model useful for future observations.

We cannot measure generalization error on every future case. Validation and test sets estimate it only when they reflect real use and are not contaminated by data leakage.

2. Underfitting, a useful fit and overfitting

Underfitting: the model is too simple, training is insufficient, or useful information is missing from the features. It misses even basic patterns, so error is high on both training and validation data.

Good generalization: the model captures stable structure. Training error is often a little lower than validation error, but the gap is moderate and both errors are acceptable.

Overfitting: the model adapts to noise, outliers and accidental correlations in one training sample. Training error becomes very low while validation error remains high or increases.

A train–validation gap alone does not prove overfitting. Check distributions, leakage, duplicates, distribution shift and label quality.

3. Model complexity and bias–variance

As complexity grows, training error often falls because the model has more freedom to fit examples. Validation error may fall at first and then rise. Its minimum is a useful guide, not a universal guarantee of an optimum.

Bias is systematic error caused by model limitations or assumptions. Variance is sensitivity to the particular training sample. A model that is too simple often has high bias; an excessively flexible one may have high variance.

This is a conceptual illustration, not a fixed law. Actual behaviour depends on data volume and quality, regularization, architecture, optimizer and evaluation procedure.

4. Reading learning curves

Learning curves show training and validation error as data volume or training steps change. If both errors are high and close, underfitting or insufficient features may be responsible. If training error is low but validation error is much higher, overfitting or a dataset mismatch may be present.

If adding representative data lowers validation error and narrows the gap, more data may help. If the curves plateau, data alone may not be enough: revisit features, task definition, architecture and labels.

Do not diagnose a model from one plot without context: curves depend on the metric, split, noise and experimental procedure.

5. Practical ways to improve

For overfitting: reduce complexity or parameter count; add L1/L2 regularization or weight decay; use dropout where appropriate; stop based on a validation metric (early stopping); collect more diverse data; use justified augmentation; remove leakage and duplicates.

For underfitting: inspect features and target; try a more expressive model; allow greater capacity; reduce excessive regularization; check training sufficiency and optimizer settings.

Regularization limits excessive adaptation. L2, for example, penalizes large weights. Early stopping halts training when validation performance stops improving. Neither replaces an independent test set or automatically repairs poor data.

Cross-validation (such as K-fold) repeats evaluation across multiple splits. The split must match the task: split time series by time and repeated records by entity. Random shuffling can leak information between training and validation.

6. A diagnostic workflow

First compare training and validation; inspect distributions; rule out leakage and duplicates; examine learning curves; only then change complexity, regularization or data. After choosing a solution, evaluate once on the test set. If you change the model afterwards, that test is no longer an independent confirmation.

Example: randomly splitting hourly energy records may put adjacent hours from the same building in both training and test sets. To assess transfer to new buildings, split by building; to forecast the future, split by time. The split should reflect the real question.

7. Comparing three models

Model A has high error on both training and validation data. Model B has low training error and moderate validation error. Model C nearly perfectly fits training data but performs poorly on validation. With comparable sets, A is likely underfitting, B generalizes better, and C is overfitting.

What next? For A, inspect features and increase capacity. For B, keep the solution and run the final test. For C, check leakage, reduce complexity or strengthen regularization, then evaluate again on validation.

Self-check: generalization and overfitting

Question 1

Training error = 4%, validation error = 22%. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Question 2

Training error = 19%, validation error = 21%; both are unacceptable. What should you inspect first?

Question 3

After representative data is added, validation error steadily decreases. What conclusion is justified?

Question 4

Can you repeatedly tune hyperparameters on the test set and still treat its score as independent?

Key terms

Generalization
Maintaining performance on new examples.
Underfitting
Failure to capture the underlying pattern.
Overfitting
Adapting to training data in a way that harms performance on new data.
Bias
Systematic error caused by model limitations.
Variance
Sensitivity to the particular training sample.
Regularization
Constraining complexity or parameter magnitude.
Early stopping
Stopping based on validation performance.
Cross-validation
Evaluation across multiple data splits.
Key takeaway. A good model is not the one that memorizes the training set best; it is the one that works reliably on new data. Compare training and validation, verify the split, diagnose the source of error, and keep the test set for a final independent evaluation.
